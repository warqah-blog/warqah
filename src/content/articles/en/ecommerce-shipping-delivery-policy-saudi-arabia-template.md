A customer can trust the product and reach checkout, then leave because three basic questions remain unanswered: **When will my order arrive? What will delivery cost? What happens if I am unavailable?**

A shipping policy should answer those questions before they turn into abandoned carts or support contacts. It is not a decorative legal page. It is the written connection between the promise shown by the store and the delivery operation behind it.

## Why every ecommerce store needs a clear shipping policy

A policy reduces uncertainty before and after purchase. It aligns the website, customer service and fulfilment team, separates processing time from transit time and defines what happens after an incorrect address, no answer or delay.

Clarity also supports compliance. Saudi Ministry of Commerce ecommerce criteria highlight disclosure of the specified delivery time before a customer completes a purchase. The Ministry's consumer guide states that the electronic invoice should include delivery fees, payment and delivery arrangements, and the delivery date and location.

## The merchant problem: multiple needs and multiple carriers

Orders do not all move at one speed or to one place. One may be urgent in Riyadh, another can arrive later the same day and a third is travelling to another Saudi city.

Under a fragmented model, a merchant may need:

- one provider for very fast delivery;
- another for standard city delivery;
- a third for intercity shipping;
- separate dashboards, agreements, invoices and support channels;
- manual decisions and duplicate data entry for every order.

Multiple logistics routes are normal. The real burden appears when the **merchant must manage all of that complexity directly**.

## 1. Separate processing time from delivery time

Processing covers payment confirmation, picking, checking, packing and label preparation. Delivery time begins at the specific point defined by the store after the parcel becomes ready and enters the delivery operation.

If the store needs two days to prepare an item, “delivery within 24 hours” should not imply that the entire order arrives one day after checkout. A better statement is:

> We prepare orders within one business day. Transit time starts after preparation and handover and varies by destination and selected service.

State whether weekends and public holidays count and identify the daily cut-off time.

## 3. Disclose shipping fees before confirmation

Tell customers how fees are calculated. Are they fixed, or do they change with destination, weight or speed? Is there a free-shipping threshold? Does COD add a fee?

A clear statement is:

> Final shipping fees appear at checkout before order confirmation and may vary by delivery address, selected service and shipment characteristics. Any eligible free-shipping offer is applied before payment.

If pricing varies under the merchant agreement, do not publish a fixed figure that may become inaccurate.

## 4. Define when the delivery promise starts

The clock might start at payment confirmation, completion of packing, carrier handover or the next business morning for orders placed after cut-off. Choose one definition the team can measure and use it consistently.

Custom-made products and pre-orders need a separate preparation statement from stocked products.

## 5. Explain tracking and notifications

Tell the customer where tracking appears, how it is sent and which contact data is used:

> After handover, we send tracking information to the phone number or email registered with the order. The customer is responsible for checking contact details and following delivery messages.

ZAJEX presents shipment tracking from pickup through arrival. Its usefulness still depends on a correct mobile number and complete address at checkout.

## 6. Set an address-change rule

Changing an address after dispatch can affect route, fee and duration and may not always be possible. The policy should state the final point for requesting a change, the authorised channel, possible additional cost and that a request is not effective until confirmed by the store.

## 7. Define failed delivery

No answer, an incorrect number, an incomplete address, refusal and rescheduling can all prevent completion. Answer these questions in advance:

- How many attempts are available?
- Will the driver contact the recipient?
- What follows a failed attempt?
- Can redelivery or return fees apply?
- When does the parcel return to the merchant?

Use language that matches the actual account agreement:

> If delivery fails because the recipient does not answer or the supplied details are incorrect, another attempt may be scheduled when the shipment and service are eligible. Redelivery or reshipping fees may apply. We will explain the available action before proceeding.

## 8. Treat COD separately

Cash on delivery needs its own fee, accepted payment method and refusal terms. Delivery coverage does not automatically mean identical COD availability in every destination. Confirm city eligibility, collection limits, settlement cycle and account-specific terms before publishing them.

## 9. Cover damaged or missing parcels

Name the reporting channel, deadline and evidence required, such as order number and packaging photographs. Distinguish delivery damage from a product defect, fulfilment shortage or wrong item. Each has a different owner and remedy.

## 10. Connect shipping to the returns policy

The shipping policy covers initial delivery and failure. The returns policy covers what happens after receipt. Link the pages and state who pays reverse-shipping cost, when return shipping is free due to merchant error, whether original delivery fees are refundable and how the return is requested.

## Common shipping-policy mistakes

### Saying only “fast delivery”

Speed is relative. Publish a duration and define its starting point.

### Copying another store

Its warehouse, preparation, provider, prices and working days may be different.

### Using one promise for every city

Riyadh delivery and intercity shipping are not equivalent. Show an appropriate promise for each route.

### Hiding COD or redelivery fees

Unexpected fees weaken trust. Explain calculation before confirmation whenever possible.

### Ignoring merchant responsibility

A delivery provider cannot repair a missing address or pack a late product. Measure preparation and data accuracy alongside transit time.

### Publishing a promise checkout cannot support

If the policy says four hours while checkout offers only an undefined shipping option, the journey is inconsistent. Test it end to end.

## Pre-publication checklist

- Is processing time separate from transit time?
- Are Riyadh and other cities separated?
- Is every available speed explained?
- Are fees visible before confirmation?
- Is the start of the delivery clock defined?
- Are tracking and address changes covered?
- Are failed attempts and redelivery covered?
- Are COD terms separate?
- Is the returns policy linked?
- Has every promise been tested with a live order?
- Does customer service use the same wording?
- Is the latest-update date visible?

## Conclusion

A successful shipping policy does not promise the fastest possible delivery everywhere. It makes the right promise for the actual order and address, then explains both the normal route and its exceptions.

Start with coverage, separate processing from transit, and define time, fees, tracking, COD, failed delivery and returns. If multiple needs force the store to manage several companies, dashboards and processes, a unified operating experience is the more valuable outcome.
